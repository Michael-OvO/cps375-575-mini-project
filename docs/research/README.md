# Curated Data Beats Clever Training Tricks

The winning strategy for a token-limited LoRA fine-tune of a ~9B base model on instruction following, competition math and Python functions is a single mixed-data SFT run on teacher-written, format-exact, per-skill data, with hyperparameters left near defaults and decisions made on held-out proxies rather than on the public benchmarks. Every comparable competition was won on data curation with off-the-shelf LoRA tooling: the two NeurIPS 2023 Efficiency Challenge winners used adapter settings at opposite extremes (rank 128 at lr 2e-5 for 3 epochs versus rank 8 at lr 5e-5 for 1 epoch), and the 4090-track winner placed 20th of 57 on public tasks but 1st on hidden ones after deliberately avoiding public-benchmark training data ([Birbal paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02247), [Percent-BFD slides](https://neurips.cc/media/neurips-2023/Slides/83962.pdf)). The three skills respond very differently to training tokens: a ~3% instruction-following slice moved IFEval by about 19 points in Tulu 3, math needs volume and scales roughly log-linearly with unique questions, and MBPP typically moves only 2 to 7 points at 7B regardless of pipeline ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2), [OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560), [OpenCodeInstruct](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04030v2)). Scorer-compatible output format is the cheapest lever in all three: robust answer parsing alone raised MATH scores by 4.66 points on average across 3,751 models, IFEval strict and loose scores differ by 12 points for one fine-tuned 8B student, and the MBPP harness executes raw prose as code if no fenced block is found ([Math-Verify blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/math_verify_leaderboard), [AutoIF](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13542v3), [inspect_evals mbpp.py](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_evals/blob/main/src/inspect_evals/mbpp/mbpp.py)). RL with verifiable rewards is a polish step at this scale (+1.3 IFEval, +1.7 MATH over 300,000 episodes in Tulu 3) and, run on instruction following alone, costs general quality. The evidence is strong on data levers and weak on three questions that matter here: no source ablates mixture proportions at fixed tokens, none tests short versus long rationales at equal tokens, and none measures these recipes under LoRA at small budgets.

## Competitions were won on data mixtures, and public scores did not predict hidden ones

The NeurIPS 2023 LLM Efficiency Challenge is the closest precedent: fine-tune an open base model on one GPU in 24 hours, scored one-third on open tasks and two-thirds on hidden tasks. The organisers' retrospective found that **open-versus-hidden score correlation was about -0.08 on the 4090 track and 0.18 on the A100 track**, that the winners did not top the open leaderboard, and that "none of them created custom code for fine-tuning" ([analysis paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13507)). They caution that low agreement reflects either overfitting or genuinely different skills, and the paper does not settle which.

Team Upaya's Birbal (Mistral-7B, 4-bit QLoRA, about 16 hours) is the cleanest case study. The team wrote that "to excel in the latter, minimize reliance on HELM-sourced data", then built a 200K-example mixture with explicit quotas per task family: 50K exact-match and 50K generation tasks from Natural Instructions, 50K MathInstruct, 25K Open-Platypus and small QA and summarisation slices. Its selection rule transfers directly: score the untouched base model few-shot on each candidate task, drop tasks that are hopeless, and oversample tasks that are weak but learnable. Birbal scored 0.52 open, 0.61 closed and 0.58 final, against 0.63, 0.32 and 0.42 for the Qwen-14B runner-up ([Birbal paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02247)).

Birbal's size ablation shows per-skill tension rather than one optimum. Going from 200K examples for 3 epochs to 400K for 2 epochs raised GSM8K from 0.44 to 0.61 but dropped TruthfulQA from 0.59 to 0.41 and BBQ from 0.74 to 0.67, and BBQ fell below the untouched base model (0.80) in every fine-tuned variant. **MATH with chain of thought moved from 0.02 to 0.12 at every dataset size**, so quadrupling math data added nothing after the first jump; the notes read this as format learning rather than new ability, which is an inference, not a measured result ([Birbal paper, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02247)).

The A100 winner complicates the "avoid public data" lesson. Percent-BFD (Qwen-14B, LoRA rank 8, alpha 32, lr 5e-5, 1 epoch) trained directly on MMLU, BIG-bench, TruthfulQA and GSM8K data and still won its track. Its iteration log is the more reusable part: sources were added one at a time against a fixed evaluation and removed when they showed "no significant effect", as happened with MetaMathQA, which also pushed training to 30 hours. The team duplicated the 1K-example LIMA set 10 times while subsampling large sets to 10% ([Percent-BFD slides](https://neurips.cc/media/neurips-2023/Slides/83962.pdf)). The treatment of public-benchmark data is therefore a genuine conflict between two winners, and the risk-weighted reading favours Birbal's approach when hidden tasks carry weight.

The AI Mathematical Olympiad winners add the math-specific precedent. Both started from base, not instruct, checkpoints (DeepSeekMath-7B-base, Qwen2.5-14B-Base) and imposed their own template through SFT. Only the chain-of-thought stage transfers to one greedy sample without tools: NuminaMath's stage-1 model reached 56.3% on MATH zero-shot greedy against 46.8% and 51.7% for the vendor's instruct and RL tunes, while its tool-using model needed 48 samples and voting for its headline score ([Numina write-up](https://huggingface.co/blog/winning-aimo-progress-prize)). Greedy trails voting sharply on hard problems (AIME 2024: 5/30 greedy versus 10/30 with 64 votes), so olympiad-level data is poor value for a greedy MATH-level target ([NuminaMath-7B-TIR card](https://huggingface.co/AI-MO/NuminaMath-7B-TIR)). Both teams did full fine-tuning on hundreds of thousands to millions of examples, so their learning rates and volumes do not transfer; no AIMO team reported a LoRA variant.

Three procedural lessons recur. Numina validated on four internal sets and measured 1 to 3% seed-to-seed variance, so smaller differences should not drive decisions ([Numina write-up](https://huggingface.co/blog/winning-aimo-progress-prize)). Model merging was fragile: DARE and TIES merges caused "significant regressions" for Numina, and NVIDIA found mixed-data training and sequential training gave similar accuracy, which argues for one mixed run over merged per-skill adapters ([AIMO-2 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16891v1)). Operational failures eliminated entries: about 30% of NeurIPS submissions timed out and more than half failed to build ([analysis paper mirror](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2503.13507)). No public write-up of a joint IFEval, MATH and MBPP contest was found, and neither competition graded code generation or verifiable constraints.

## A 3% instruction-following slice buys ~19 IFEval points, but only for constraints it has seen

Instruction following is the most token-efficient of the three skills. Tulu 3's persona-driven set holds 29,980 GPT-4o-written prompts, each carrying 1 to 3 constraints from IFEval's 25-type taxonomy, about 3% of a 939K-prompt mixture. One extraction of the leave-one-out table shows **IFEval falling from 72.8 to 53.6 when the persona data is removed**; a second extraction of the same paper could not recover that cell, so the exact figure needs a check against the PDF, though the direction is confirmed in the text ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2)).

That gain is narrow. Tulu 3's 8B model scores 82.4 on IFEval but 24.3 on the paper's out-of-distribution constraints and 28.9 on IFBench's 58 unseen constraints; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct scores 74.7 and 31.3 ([IFBench paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02833v3)). For a grade that includes hidden instruction-following benchmarks, this is the central finding: training on the IFEval taxonomy alone teaches the taxonomy.

The IFBench ablations identify what generalises. Diversity of constraint types and compositions matters; volume per constraint does not, since accuracy is flat from 10 to 1,000 instances per constraint. Multi-constraint prompts beat single-constraint ones on both benchmarks (IFEval 71.2 with one constraint per prompt versus 85.8 with six; IFBench peaks at three). Wide numeric ranges for parameters such as word counts match or beat narrow ones, and ranges disjoint from the test are worst. Removing length or keyword constraints from training hurts IFEval most, while case and format constraints are nearly free. The paper adds 29 hand-written training constraints (IFTrain) to IFEval's 25 ([IFBench paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02833v3)). Conifer and FollowBench agree that stacked constraints are the hard case: Conifer's 7B model falls from 54.3 with one constraint to 30.5 with five ([Conifer](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02823v1)).

Teacher quality dominates training algorithm. In AutoIF, switching the supervisor from Qwen2-72B to GPT-4 raised a 7B student's loose prompt accuracy from 46.6 to 59.5, while moving from SFT to online DPO with a fixed supervisor added about 2 points. A fraction of 1/64 of the GPT-4-supervised preference data already delivered +11.4. AutoIF's verification filters were worth 2.2 points in total ([AutoIF](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13542v3)). The strict-versus-loose gap is diagnostic: a LLaMA3-8B student scored 28.7 strict against 40.3 loose after SFT, and loose scoring differs only by stripping markdown emphasis and the first or last line, so preambles, sign-offs and decorative markdown account for the gap ([IFEval paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.07911)).

RL with verifiable rewards has a wide range of outcomes. Tulu 3's mixed-task, KL-regularised PPO over GSM8K, MATH and constraint prompts added +1.3 IFEval, +3.3 GSM8K and +1.7 MATH at 8B over 300,000 episodes with side effects under 2 points; the larger jump came from preference tuning (72.8 to 81.1 IFEval) ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v5)). IFBench's GRPO recipe with no KL penalty is the aggressive end: it lifted a Qwen2.5-7B base model to 87.8 IFEval and 53.7 IFBench, and beat verified DPO by about 9 IFEval points on the same prompts, but trained from a base model with a constraint-only reward it left GSM8K at 15.3; from the Tulu DPO checkpoint it cut AlpacaEval 2 from 34.5 to 21.3 and GSM8K from 87.6 to 83.2 ([IFBench paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02833v3)). Two IFBench tables disagree (89.65 / 30.6 for "GRPO after DPO" versus 92.2 / 44.6 for the headline row), and the cause is unconfirmed. No head-to-head of RL against more rejection-sampled SFT at equal compute exists.

## Math rewards new questions, a strong teacher, and concise worked solutions

Math is the skill that needs volume, and the literature agrees on where volume should go. OpenMathInstruct-2, which distilled Llama3.1-405B into a Llama3.1-8B base student with text-only chain of thought, found that **at a fixed 256K pairs, cutting unique questions from 6.5K to 1K cost more than 10 points**. MetaMath saw +0.1% from 20K more solutions to existing questions against +2.3 to +2.6% from 20K new bootstrapped questions, and MuggleMath found 7B students stop improving at about three responses per query ([OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560), [MetaMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.12284), [MuggleMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05506)). Synthetic questions seeded from MATH training problems are nearly as good as real ones per example (7.9 versus 8.4 at 7.5K) and scale roughly log-linearly to 480K ([Xwin-Math](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04706)). Grade-school word-problem augmentation gives "little to no help" on MATH ([MuggleMath](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05506)).

A stronger teacher is better at this student size. An 8B student scored 37.9 with a 405B teacher against 30.1 with an 8B teacher in the same pipeline, and a Qwen2.5-7B student scored 71.3 with a 72B teacher against 63.6 with a 3B teacher; the "teacher too strong" effect appears only at 3B and below ([OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560), [learnability-gap paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12143)).

Rationale length is the main conflict in the math evidence. Concise solutions beat verbose ones from the same teacher (44.5 at 237 mean tokens versus 40.6 at 331 tokens) on Llama3.1-8B ([OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560)). Yet reasoning-model traces averaging 3,385 tokens beat 576-token solutions on a Llama-3.1-8B student (50.0 versus 44.6) and tied on Qwen2.5-7B (68.2 versus 67.8), with only 7.5K training problems and different teachers ([learnability-gap paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12143)). The two results compare different length regimes and are compatible with an interior optimum of complete steps without filler. Because long traces cost about six times the tokens for a neutral-to-positive effect, concise solutions are the better trade under a token budget, but no paper ran the equal-token comparison.

Filtering solutions buys little, and selection bias matters more than label noise. LLM-judge filters removed 6 to 12% of OpenMathInstruct-2 data with no gain (43.6 unfiltered versus 43.0 to 43.8 filtered), the student tolerated up to 20% wrong solutions at 256K scale, and requiring higher teacher self-agreement on synthetic questions lowered accuracy from 50.1 to 42.0 because it discards hard items ([OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560)). Answer matching against a known reference is free and universal among winners. DART-Math shows the bias problem: in one standard augmentation set, 51.1% of Level 5 training problems received no solutions, and fixing coverage of hard problems raised Llama3-8B from 39.7 to 45.3 (uniform) or 46.6 (difficulty-proportional) on MATH at equal size, at a cost of 1.4 points on GSM8K. The gain is about 1 point for models already strong at math ([DART-Math](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.13690)).

Contamination and format need active handling. Despite seeding only from training problems, **about 9% of OpenMathInstruct-2's synthetic MATH-style questions were removed as paraphrases of test problems** ([OpenMathInstruct-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01560)). On format, each successful pipeline enforces one rigid final-answer marker and drops non-conforming samples; OpenMathInstruct-2 truncates after the first boxed sentence and discards solutions with multiple boxed answers, and found that a base template without role tokens cut reversion to verbose style from 57% to 0.1%. Extraction, parsing and equivalence failures (answers buried in sentences, decimals for fractions, "k = 1" versus "1", intervals) cost several points and concentrate in easy categories such as Algebra (+8.27 under a better parser) ([Math-Verify blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/math_verify_leaderboard)). No source ablates boxed versus an "ANSWER:" line; the scorer decides.

Absolute numbers depend mostly on the base model. The same roughly 0.5M-sample recipe gives about 20% on LLaMA-2-7B, 45% on Mistral-7B or Llama3-8B and 54% on DeepSeekMath-7B, and on the Tulu 3 mixture Qwen 2.5 7B reached 49.4 MATH against 31.5 for Llama 3.1 8B ([DART-Math](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.13690), [Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2)). A 2026 re-examination reports that chain-of-thought distillation often degraded already-strong instruct students below their own baseline, with per-benchmark values available only in figures ([capacity-gap revisit](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08880v1)).

## MBPP gains are single digits, and the harness format decides a share of them

Code generation offers the least headroom. Across four independent pipelines, a ~7B model gains 15 to 21 points on HumanEval-style benchmarks but only 2 to 7 on MBPP: +7.2 MBPP+ for Magicoder on CodeLlama-7B, +2.2 MBPP for WaveCoder on DeepSeekCoder-6.7B, +5.0 MBPP+ for SelfCodeAlign on CodeQwen1.5-7B, and +3.2 MBPP+ for 5M OpenCodeInstruct samples on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct. A general 8B model has more room: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct rose from 68.3 to 77.5 on MBPP ([Magicoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.02120), [WaveCoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.14187v4), [SelfCodeAlign](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.24198), [OpenCodeInstruct](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04030v2)). Numbers labelled "MBPP" are not comparable across papers because split, shot count and decoding differ.

The supported data design is plain. Natural-language-to-code instructions beat code-to-code by 1.5 to 3.4 MBPP points. Seed type (competitive-programming versus generic functions) leaves MBPP flat at 83.1 to 83.3. Extra rounds of complexity evolution stop helping after one round (HumanEval 56.1 after round 1, 51.2 after round 4). Raw comment-function pairs without instruction framing give no gain at all (34.1, equal to base) ([OpenCodeInstruct](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04030v2), [WizardCoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08568), [Magicoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.02120)). Breadth of simple, distinct function tasks matters more than difficulty.

Execution filtering conflicts across sources in a way that tracks the generator's error rate. It was worth +3.6 HumanEval+ when a 7B model generated its own data and only 24% of samples passed, but +0.3 to +0.4 MBPP when a 32B teacher's samples already passed 72% of the time; in that comparison an LLM judge score of 5 did better (82.3 versus 81.4 MBPP), and training on failures only cost about 1 point, which implies many "failures" were correct code paired with wrong generated tests ([SelfCodeAlign](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.24198), [OpenCodeInstruct](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04030v2)). With a strong teacher, test-passing is cheap insurance rather than a major lever. No source tests having a second model write the tests.

The inspect_evals MBPP task, read from source, shows what the format must be. The prompt is three-shot, includes the test asserts, and instructs "just output the Python function"; the scorer extracts a fenced Python block, **falls back to executing the entire raw completion if none is found**, appends the asserts and passes only on a clean process exit within 30 seconds. Its default is temperature 0.5 over 5 epochs, so a greedy single-sample run needs explicit overrides. The few-shot examples come from MBPP's prompt split, which matters if MBPP data is off-limits for training ([inspect_evals mbpp.py](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_evals/blob/main/src/inspect_evals/mbpp/mbpp.py)). Prose without a fence, a function name that differs from the asserts, or trailing demo code that raises all fail on format alone. Because the asserts carry the function name and call signature, training items shaped as a short description plus three asserts, answered with exactly one fenced block containing only the function, match the harness; this follows from the source code and no paper tests it. How the extractor handles multiple or unlabeled fences is unconfirmed. No paper ablates explanation-before-code against code-only for this model size.

RL with unit-test rewards has not beaten SFT at 7B: the one 7B figure found is 62.4 to 65.4 on MBPP, seen only in a search snippet, while gains of 13 to 19 points are confined to models under 2B ([process-supervised RL paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.17621)).

## LoRA defaults are safe, learning rate is the one sweep worth paying for

LoRA matches full fine-tuning when adapter capacity exceeds the information in the dataset, which covers typical post-training datasets. The supported defaults are adapters on all linear layers, since attention-only at rank 256 lost to MLP-only at rank 128 with matched parameters; a learning rate about 10 times the full fine-tuning rate (fitted multiplier 9.8 across 14 models); and small-to-moderate batches, because LoRA tolerates large batches worse than full fine-tuning and higher rank does not fix it ([LoRA Without Regret](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). Tinker's guidance is rank 32 by default, an adapter parameter count at least equal to the number of weighted completion tokens, and a sweep over learning rates of 1e-4 to 1e-3 on held-out loss; its reference runs on Tulu 3 data found optima of 4e-4 to 1e-3 at batch 128 ([Tinker LoRA primer](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/lora-primer/), [Tinker hyperparameter tutorial](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tutorials/advanced/sl-hyperparams/)).

Rank is the conflict. Thinking Machines reports that rank 32 suffices and that the optimal learning rate changes by less than 2x between rank 4 and 512 under a fixed alpha of 32. Biderman et al. saw HumanEval rise monotonically with rank on Llama-2-7B after code tuning (0.358 at rank 16, 0.417 at rank 64, 0.498 at rank 256) and called alpha = 2r "crucial", while math was nearly rank-insensitive (GSM8K 0.580, 0.624, 0.625) ([LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)). The studies differ in alpha convention, optimizer and epoch count, so learning rates must not be copied across conventions; a rank 32 versus 128 run at the same learning rate resolves it cheaply. The Tulu 2 QLoRA result that trailed full fine-tuning at 7B (GSM8K 20.5 versus 34.0) used a low alpha-to-rank ratio of 0.25 and is plausibly under-tuned rather than a LoRA limit ([Tulu 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10702)).

Epochs should be few. Tulu 3 found 2 epochs best, LoRA's HumanEval peaked at epoch 4 and declined by epoch 8, Raschka saw a second epoch hurt on Alpaca, and Birbal's best checkpoint came at epoch 2 of 3 ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2), [Biderman et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2), [Raschka](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/practical-tips-for-finetuning-llms)). Schedule and warmup are low priority: the Thinking Machines results used a constant rate with no warmup.

On mixing, the Tulu 3 leave-one-out table shows skills are mostly additive in one 8B model. Removing math data cut MATH from 31.5 to 23.5 and GSM8K from 76.2 to 64.1 while IFEval moved 2 points; removing general chat data (WildChat) cut AlpacaEval 2 from 12.4 to 7.5 and IFEval by 2.7 but left math unchanged. The larger risk is a missing skill slice, not a conflicting one ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2)). Bulk low-quality instruction data is the exception: full Alpaca tuning lowered Llama-2-7B's GSM8K from 11.50 to 7.00, while a 1K longest-response subset raised it to 14.50 ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.06094v1)). All Tulu mixture evidence is full fine-tuning at 939K prompts; LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning on out-of-domain probes (0.517 versus 0.414 after 16 code epochs), which plausibly lowers interference further but is untested on a mixture ([Biderman et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)).

Failure-driven data has a measured recipe from LLM2LLM, on Llama-2-7B with full fine-tuning. At equal augmented volume, ten iterative rounds beat one-shot generation (23.73 versus 16.30 on GSM8K from 100 seeds), generating only from real seed errors beat re-augmenting synthetic examples (23.75 versus 18.32), and **retraining from the base checkpoint each round beat continued fine-tuning (23.75 versus 14.71)**. Gains shrink as real data grows, from +18.6 points at 74 seeds to +5.2 at the full 7.5K ([LLM2LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15042)). Retraining each round multiplies training tokens, so round count is the cost lever. Claims that accumulating rather than replacing data prevents collapse, and that self-improvement yields in-domain gains with weaker generalisation, come from secondary summaries and are unconfirmed.

For a base checkpoint trained through a chat template, the template itself is a roughly 1-point effect (51.6 to 53.0 across five templates in Tulu 3), while stop-token handling breaks runs: when pad equals EOS, common collators mask every EOS label and the model never learns to stop, and Qwen3 base tokenizers set EOS to a different token than the one the chat template emits ([Tulu 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15124v2), [HF forum write-up](https://huggingface.co/datasets/John6666/forum2/blob/main/qwen_eos_token_1.md), [The Kaitchup](https://kaitchup.substack.com/p/qwen3-when-im_end-suddenly-becomes)). These are practitioner reports, not controlled experiments. The Tinker cookbook separately warns that system prompt, max tokens, temperature and timeout "can shift results significantly" on these exact benchmarks ([tinker-cookbook](https://github.com/thinking-machines-lab/tinker-cookbook)).

## Conclusion: pipeline priorities ranked by expected impact

The evidence orders the design decisions clearly, and the order is not the one that effort usually follows. The table ranks them by expected effect on the combined score, with the hidden-benchmark share in mind.

| Rank | Action | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make every training target match the grader's interface: one bare final answer in the scorer's marker for math, one fenced code block containing only the function with the name taken from the asserts, direct answers without preamble or decorative markdown for instructions, and a supervised turn-end token on every example | Costs no extra tokens; measured losses of 4 to 12 points per benchmark; stop-token failures break greedy runs outright |
| 2 | Build a teacher-written instruction-following slice with 2 to 6 stacked constraints per prompt, a constraint set wider than IFEval's 25 types, and wide numeric ranges | Most points per token (about 19 IFEval from 3% of a mix); type diversity is the only measured route to unseen constraints |
| 3 | Spend the bulk of tokens on math: new teacher-written MATH-style questions with one concise solution each, plus two or three distinct solutions for hard problems; skip judge filtering, keep answer matching | Math is the only skill that keeps scaling with volume; unique questions and hard-problem coverage are the measured levers |
| 4 | Treat the three public benchmarks as format and difficulty references, not training sources, and select checkpoints on several held-out proxies with seed variance measured | The only mechanism with competition evidence for hidden-set ranking; also guards against the 9% paraphrase leakage seen in synthetic math |
| 5 | Add a modest slice of simple NL-to-function tasks with asserts in the prompt and code-only answers; keep test-passing solutions | Single-digit ceiling, so a small slice captures most of it; format compliance is a share of the gain |
| 6 | Train one mixed run with LoRA on all layers at rank 32 to 64, sweep only the learning rate, and stop at 1 to 2 epochs on held-out loss | Defaults are well supported; unique data beats extra epochs under a token cap |
| 7 | Run one or two failure-driven rounds: find real prompts the student fails, have the teacher write similar new ones, retrain from base | Measured gains are single digits once the pool is large, and each round re-spends training tokens |
| 8 | Consider RL with verifiable rewards last, mixed-task and KL-regularised | +1 to +3 points at 8B in the safe configuration; the aggressive one trades away the other two skills |

Two implications go beyond any single source. First, the hidden-benchmark component changes the instruction-following target more than the other two: MATH-style and MBPP-style hidden sets reward the same underlying skill as the public ones, but a hidden constraint-following benchmark rewards exactly what IFEval-taxonomy training fails to provide, so constraint diversity is where the ranking is most at stake. Second, the highest-value experiments are the ones the literature has not run, and all are cheap: the same model trained at two or three mixture ratios at fixed tokens, concise versus long math rationales at equal tokens, and rank 32 versus 128 at one learning rate. Every candidate should be compared against the untouched base model on each skill separately, because fine-tuning in these studies repeatedly lowered abilities the base model already had (BBQ in Birbal, GSM8K after Alpaca tuning, strong instruct students after distillation).
